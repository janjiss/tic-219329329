TIC 219329329.01

A Jupiter-size object crosses a Sun-like star 1,800 light-years away every 3.48 days. It passes every false-positive test run so far. Its mass has not been measured, so it is a candidate, not a confirmed planet.

What would confirm it. A planet, a brown dwarf and the smallest stars are all about this size. Only a mass from radial-velocity spectra can tell them apart: a planet would move the star by about 126 m/s per Jupiter mass, a brown dwarf or small star by several km/s.

The signal

The star dims by about 1% for two hours, every 3.4816 days, in every sector TESS has observed it.

E1

The folded transit

DetectedCopy link

51,810 brightness measurements from six sectors, stacked on the 3.4816-day period. The dip is 0.97% deep and lasts 2.09 ± 0.05 hours.

The fitted model is a planet-size body crossing near the edge of the star (impact parameter 0.87 ± 0.02), which is why the dip has sloping sides and a short flat bottom.

Blue points are 10-minute averages with their uncertainties; grey dots are a sample of individual 200-second measurements. Photometry is my own 3×3 pixel aperture on the full-frame images.

Is the dip on this star?

TESS pixels are 21″ wide, so a faint eclipsing binary nearby can imitate a planet on the bright star. These five tests ask where the dip comes from.

E4

Difference images

PassCopy link

Subtracting in-transit images from out-of-transit images leaves a single point of lost light, on the target. Its centre is within about 1″ of the star in five of six sectors.

Sector 102 is 7.8 ± 4.1″ off (1.9σ). There the wing of a bright star 263″ away crosses the corner of the image; its own "dip" is a single event, not periodic.

Neighbouring stars

PassCopy link

Gaia DR3 lists no star within 22″. The nearest is about 1,400 times fainter in the TESS band and adds 0.07% to the target's light.

Everything within 63″ adds up to 0.28% of the target's light. A star must supply at least 0.97% to cause a 0.97% dip, even if it vanished completely. No known neighbour can.

E7

Depth against reduction choices

One anomalyCopy link

Changing the detrending window from 0.5 to 3 days moves the depth by about 1%. The official TESS-SPOC light curve and my own photometry agree to within a few percent.

The QLP pipeline's light curves run about 10% shallow, and its small-aperture product is far shallower and changes from sector to sector (3100–7200 ppm). My own 2×2 pixel photometry does not show this, so it is probably a normalisation effect in that product. This has not been confirmed.

Stellar density from the transit shape

PassCopy link

The duration and shape of a transit measure the density of the star being crossed. With no prior knowledge of the star, the fit returns 0.61–0.73 times the Sun's density. Catalogues give 0.56–0.65 for this star.

A dip on a smaller, hidden star would return a density several times higher.

Is it two stars eclipsing?

An eclipsing pair of stars leaves fingerprints a planet does not: alternating eclipse depths, a second eclipse half an orbit later, and a star stretched by its partner's gravity.

E9

PassCopy link

Two unequal stars at twice the period would give alternating deep and shallow eclipses.

E10

No secondary eclipse

PassCopy link

Half an orbit after the transit, the brightness changes by −196 ± 130 ppm: no eclipse. The 3σ upper limit is about 390 ppm.

A companion star would be hidden behind the primary at that moment and its light would drop out. The limit rules out a companion that shines; it does not rule out a very cool one.

E11

No tidal distortion: a mass limit

Mass limitCopy link

A massive companion stretches the star, which then brightens and fades twice per orbit. The measured amplitude is 5 ± 54 ppm. The 3σ limit of 167 ppm caps the companion at 0.083 solar masses, or 87 Jupiter masses.

This excludes ordinary stellar companions. It does not separate a planet from a brown dwarf or from a star right at the bottom of the main sequence. The once-per-orbit terms are dominated by instrument systematics and were not used.

PassCopy link

The period was fitted on sectors 68–104 only. The six transits in sector 105, not used in that fit, arrive −0.7 ± 1.3 minutes from the predicted times.

No other signals

PassCopy link

With the transits masked, a second search finds no other periodic dip. The star shows no brightness variation at the orbital period or at half of it.

How likely is a false positive?

TRICERATOPS compares how well a planet and each false-positive scenario explain the light curve, weighted by how common each is.

E15

False-positive probability

Likely planetCopy link

The false-positive probability is about 6% (median 0.057 on both light curves). The probability that a known neighbouring star is responsible is 0.

Statistical validation needs a false-positive probability below 1.5%. This candidate falls in the "likely planet" class. All of the remaining false-positive weight sits in scenarios with an unseen companion star bound to the target, which high-resolution imaging would test. TRICERATOPS does not model brown dwarfs.

A later calculation that also uses the rest of the orbit gives about 3–4% (E17).

E16

Independent detection from the ground

DetectedCopy link

ASAS-SN, a network of small ground-based telescopes, measured this star 2,883 times between 2014 and 2025. Folded on the TESS period, its data show a dip of 8072 ± 1928 ppm (4.2σ) at the predicted time.

This is a different instrument, in blue-green light, with a 16″ aperture that is smaller than one TESS pixel. The depth is 0.89 ± 0.21 of what a planet on the target star predicts in those bands, so there is no sign that the depth changes with colour. The test is too weak to exclude a slightly redder host. The period holds for 1,258 orbits before the TESS reference epoch.

Half-hour averages of 2,865 ASAS-SN measurements, with the transit model for each band. Uncertainties are large because only 76 measurements fall in transit.

E17

False-positive probability with the whole orbit included

Likely planetCopy link

Adding the missing secondary eclipse (E10) and the missing tidal distortion (E11) to every eclipsing-binary scenario lowers the false-positive probability from about 6% to about 3–4%. That is still above the 1.5% needed for validation.

TRICERATOPS fits only the hours around the transit. A star doing the eclipsing would also be hidden half an orbit later, and would stretch its host. Binaries that fit the transit predict a secondary eclipse of 400–600 ppm and a tidal signal of 90–160 ppm, and neither is seen, so those scenarios fall by factors of roughly 15 to 25.

What remains is almost entirely a planet orbiting an unseen companion star bound to the target. These limits cannot test that, and its estimate varies from run to run (0.001–0.088). This is TRICERATOPS with two added terms, not the standard procedure, and both terms were set cautiously: the secondary eclipse was entered as 0 ± 130 ppm although −196 ppm was measured, and the predicted tidal signal was reduced by about 20%.

What is not settled

  • No mass. No spectra exist in any archive searched (E18), so a brown dwarf or a star at the very bottom of the main sequence is not excluded.
  • No high-resolution imaging. A close companion star cannot be ruled out, and Gaia's RUWE of 1.27 is mildly elevated.
  • No precise transit in a second colour. ASAS-SN sees the dip in blue-green light at 4σ (E16), which is too weak to test whether the depth changes with colour.
  • QLP small-aperture anomaly. One pipeline product gives a much shallower, sector-dependent depth (E7). Direct photometry does not reproduce it, but the cause was not confirmed.
  • Sector 102 centroid. The offset there is 1.9σ (E4).
  • Sector 1 has no data. In 2018 the star fell in the gap between two detectors, so the TESS baseline starts in 2023.
  • Sector 105 time stamps. They use the detector-centre barycentric correction and can be off by up to 1.5 minutes.
  • Catalogue search is partial. The all-catalogue VizieR query failed (E14).
  • Not peer reviewed. This is an independent analysis of public data.

Follow-up that would settle it

  1. Two or three spectra near the orbit's quarter phases. A brown dwarf or small star would show several km/s; a planet about 126 m/s per Jupiter mass.
  2. High-resolution imaging (speckle or adaptive optics) to look for close companions. This would also lower the false-positive probability.
  3. A ground-based transit in a second colour. The event is 1% deep and lasts 2.1 hours.
  4. Registration as a community candidate (CTOI) on ExoFOP so follow-up groups can pick it up.
